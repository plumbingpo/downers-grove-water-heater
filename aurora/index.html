Keeping Your Aurora Water Heater Running Longer

Most early failures trace back to skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are simple. Flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, have the anode rod inspected every few years, and test the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard to protect the heat exchanger.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the manufacture date, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that point and is also showing rusty water, popping sounds, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than an emergency replacement after a leak.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and prevents a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Water heaters in and around Aurora face a familiar set of problems. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and rust starts working on the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well may see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless heater on hard water needs regular descaling. And an older installation getting a new heater may need updates that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or strapping where required. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less to address early.
